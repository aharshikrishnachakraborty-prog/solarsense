# Model card — expected-output model

_Generated by `python -m engine.expected_model` — do not edit by hand._

## What it does
For every inverter and every 15 minutes of daylight, it predicts how much AC power the inverter **should** produce given the sunlight (irradiation), panel and air temperature and time of day. Comparing this **expected power** with the **actual power** is how we spot faults.

## Data
Kaggle "Solar Power Generation Data" (2 plants in India, 22 inverters each, 15-minute readings), cleaned in Phase 2. Only daylight rows with a real inverter reading are used for training/testing.

**Time split (never random):** the model learns from the earlier days and is tested on later days it has never seen — just like real life, where we predict the future from the past.

## How to read the scores
- **MAE** — on average, how many kW the prediction is off by. Lower is better.
- **R²** — how much of the ups and downs in power the model explains. 1.0 = perfect, 0 = useless. Solar power follows sunlight very closely, so even a simple model scores high.
- **MAPE** — average error in %, only on rows where actual power ≥ 100.0 kW (a % error on a tiny number would mislead).
- "Outages excluded" shows how well the model describes **healthy** behaviour; "all rows" includes real faults on the test days, which the model is *supposed* to miss (that gap is what we detect).

## Limitations (honest)
- Only 34 days of data from one season (May–June): unknown accuracy in winter or monsoon.
- One weather sensor per plant: a cloud over one part of the plant looks like a fault there. Peer comparison (each inverter vs the plant median) reduces this.
- The Random Forest learns each inverter's "normal" from the training days. If an inverter was already degraded then, the model treats that as normal.
- Random Forests cannot predict beyond what they saw (e.g. sunlight stronger than any training day).
- Plant 1 DC values were rescaled (see assumptions.md); the models predict AC power, which was not rescaled.
